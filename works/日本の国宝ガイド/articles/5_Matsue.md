# The Castle That Had to Prove Its Own Age

*Demoted in 1950, restored to the first rank in 2015 on the evidence of two wooden boards. Matsue is the plainest of Japan's National Treasure keeps and, by some distance, the most honest.*

---

In 1950, Matsue Castle was demoted.

It had been a National Treasure since 1935. Then the Cultural Properties Protection Law reorganised the categories, the keep came out of the reshuffle as an Important Cultural Property, and there it sat for the next six decades — a rank down, and, in the way of these things, a quiet verdict. The building had not changed. The paperwork had.

The trouble was that nobody could prove when it was built. Matsue had tradition and it had timber, but it lacked the documentary spine that the new criteria wanted.

Then, in 2012, two prayer boards turned up at Matsue Shrine, in the grounds below the keep. One bore a date: an auspicious day in the first month of the sixteenth year of Keichō — 1611. Investigators matched the boards to the structure they had been written for, the construction sequence resolved, and in 2015 Matsue was re-designated a National Treasure. Sixty-three years after losing the title, it got it back, because someone found a piece of wood in a cupboard.

It is the newest of the five designations, and the only one that reads like a detective story.

## The man who served all three

The castle was begun by **Horio Yoshiharu**, a soldier with an unusually complete career: he served Nobunaga, then Hideyoshi — under whom he was one of the three senior arbitrators of the Toyotomi administration — and then Ieyasu. His son Tadauji fought well at Sekigahara, and the family was rewarded with Izumo and Oki, some 240,000 koku.

They chose Kameda-yama, a low hill by Lake Shinji, and built. Tadauji died young; Yoshiharu, an old man by then, saw the work through. The keep was finished in 1611 — and Yoshiharu died in the same year, which lends the date a certain finality.

The Horio line then failed for want of an heir in 1633. Kyōgoku Tadataka arrived in 1634 and was dead within three years; his deathbed adoption was refused and the domain was forfeit a second time. In 1638 **Matsudaira Naomasa**, a grandson of Ieyasu, took Matsue, and his house held it for ten generations until the Restoration. Three dynasties in thirty years, then two hundred and thirty years of nothing much happening: the castle got its stability late.

## One hundred and eighty yen

The abolition ordinance of 1873 priced the keep at 180 yen and put it up for sale. Rice at the time ran to about three yen a bale, so the figure is not quite as trivial as it sounds — but it is not far off. The tower was to be taken down for materials.

Two men stopped it. **Katsube Motouemon**, a wealthy local farmer — and, by the accounts that survive, a cultivated one, a writer of *waka* who practised tea and calligraphy — and **Takagi Gonpachi**, a former retainer of the domain then working as an official at a copper mine. Gonpachi went to the men he knew through his work; between them they raised the full purchase price out of their own money, paid the government, and kept the keep standing.

There is a version of Japanese heritage history in which castles survive because the state valued them. Matsue is the correction. It survives because two private individuals in a provincial town decided, at their own expense, that it should.

## The architecture of not pretending

Matsue is black. Where Himeji and Hikone are finished in white lime, this keep is clad to the eaves in dark boarding, and where they carry ornament, it carries almost none. Four roof tiers, five storeys, one basement; about thirty metres from the ground, twenty-two from the top of the stone base — which makes it the third tallest of the twelve keeps still standing. The only concession to decoration is the run of gables that gave the castle its other name, Chidori-jō, the plovers.

Everything else is problem-solving in public, and this is what makes the building worth an hour of close attention.

There was not enough good timber. So the builders dispensed with a central mast — the usual way to tie a tall keep together — and instead used short through-pillars, each spanning two floors, interlocked and staggered up the height of the tower. Of 308 pillars, 96 do this work.

And the wood they did have was substandard. So 130 of those pillars are wrapped: boarded over and clamped with iron staples and rings, a technique called *tsutsumi-ita*, to close splits and conceal knots in stock that would otherwise have been rejected. A third of the structural timber in a National Treasure is in a jacket, and has held for four hundred years.

I find this considerably more moving than a perfect building. Matsue was made from what was available by people who were not going to let that stop them, and it did not fall down.

Two further details reward the climb. The keep contains a **well** — twenty-four metres deep originally, now filled to about half — and it is the only one left in any surviving keep in Japan. Nagoya and Hamamatsu had them; those castles are gone. This is siege thinking made concrete: the assumption that you might be up here for a while.

And the entrance turret, set on stonework a step lower than the keep's own base, has an *ishiuchidana* in its basement — a boarded platform at the right height to attack anyone who reached the door. Nothing here is ornamental. Everything is a calculation about a bad day that never came.

The top floor is the reward: railed, open, no walls, the full circle. Lake Shinji on one side, the city and the hills on the other.

## Water, and what comes out of it

Matsue is a city organised around water, and the sensible way to see it is from the boats. The **Horikawa** circuit runs the old moats in a low, roofed launch, under bridges that require everyone to duck, past stone revetments, pine and the backs of samurai houses. Forty-odd minutes, and it reframes the whole town.

**Shiomi-nawate**, along the north moat, is the best surviving stretch of the castle town: an earthen-walled lane of former retainers' residences, one of which is open and preserved close to its eighteenth-century condition. Lafcadio Hearn lived on this street, and the museum and house here make the case for him as more than a curiosity — a writer who arrived in 1890, married into a samurai family and produced the ghost stories through which a great many people outside Japan first encountered the place.

Then the lake. Shinji is a brackish lagoon, shallow and wide, and its sunsets are famous within Japan in a way that requires no adjectives from me. Take the last hour of the day on the northern shore.

Eat what the lake produces. The **seven delicacies of Lake Shinji** — sea bass, *moroge* shrimp, eel, smelt, whitebait, carp and *shijimi* clams — are the local canon, and the mnemonic that runs the initials together, *sumō-ashi-koshi*, is the sort of thing people will teach you unprompted. The shijimi are the ones to insist on: brackish water grows them large and thick, and Shinji lands more of them than anywhere else in the country. A bowl of clear shijimi miso soup at breakfast is one of the great small pleasures of the San'in coast.

For lunch, **Izumo soba** — buckwheat ground with the husk on, so it arrives dark, coarse and considerably more aromatic than the pale noodles of Tokyo, served cold in stacked lacquer rounds with the dressing poured over.

## The Particulars

**Getting there** — JR to Matsue. The Lakeline loop bus from stand 7 at the north exit reaches Matsue-jō Ōtemae in about ten minutes (¥210), then eight minutes on foot. Walking the whole way takes around twenty-five.

**Admission and hours** — ¥1,200 for adults, free for schoolchildren, as of the July 2026 revision; opening hours shift with the season. Check before you go: [matsue-castle.jp](https://www.matsue-castle.jp/)

**How long** — An hour for the keep; two to three with the moat cruise; half a day with Shiomi-nawate and the Hearn house.

**When** — Autumn, for the walking and the sunsets. Winter for the clearest view from the top floor — the San'in coast pays for that clarity in grey days, so take what you are given.

**Nearby** — The Horikawa moat cruise; Shiomi-nawate and the preserved samurai residence; the Lafcadio Hearn Memorial Museum and former house; the northern shore of Lake Shinji at dusk.
