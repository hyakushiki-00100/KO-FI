# Small, Old, and Still Standing

*The least imposing of Japan's five National Treasure keeps has the best argument: a castle whose enemies were earthquakes and typhoons, rescued each time by the town at its feet.*

---

The handrail on the top floor of Inuyama's keep comes up to about mid-thigh.

You notice this the moment you step out onto the balcony that rings the fourth storey, some twenty metres above a hill that itself drops away to the Kiso River. The Nōbi plain runs south to the haze. Behind you is a room the size of a modest sitting room. In front of you is a rail that a determined ten-year-old could go over.

It is not a design fault. It is simply what a handrail was when this floor was built, several centuries before anyone thought to legislate the matter, and Inuyama has never seen the point of correcting it. You are standing in a genuinely old building that has not been made comfortable for you. Very few places in Japan give you that sensation quite so directly, and it is the reason to come.

## The oldest form

Inuyama is the smallest of the five keeps and, by most reckonings, the earliest. Tradition dates the first castle here to around 1537 and credits Oda Nobuyasu — Nobunaga's uncle — with building it, on a bluff where the Kiso divides Owari from Mino. The job was to watch the crossing. The castle's purpose and the river have never been separable.

What survives is a *bōrō-gata* keep: three roof tiers, four storeys, two basement levels. The type is easy to read once you know it. A large building with a hipped-and-gabled roof, and a watchtower set on top of it — visibly two things stacked, rather than one thing designed whole. This is what keeps looked like before the form settled down. Later castles adopted the *sōtō-gata* layer-tower plan, in which each storey steps neatly in from the one below, and the result is the composed elegance of Himeji.

Inuyama is from before that. It is the tower as a working idea rather than a finished style, and the slight awkwardness of the silhouette is the evidence.

The stonework says the same thing. The base is *nozura-zumi* — rough field stones stacked more or less as found, gaps and all. At Himeji you can watch the technique improve over decades towards tight-fitting cut blocks. Here you are looking at the stage before the improvement began.

## Why it never needed to be bigger

The obvious question about Inuyama — why so small? — has a flat answer. It didn't need to be large, because the ground was doing the work. The north face falls away in a cliff to the Kiso. There was no need to dig a moat or raise a high revetment. Cross to the far bank and look back and the whole strategic logic assembles itself in about four seconds.

Which makes the castle's one significant military episode all the more pointed. In 1584, during the Komaki–Nagakute campaign, Hideyoshi's general Ikeda Tsuneoki simply crossed the Kiso and took the place. The river you are relying on can also be used by the other side. Terrain is not loyalty.

## Earthquake, typhoon, and a family that paid

After the Restoration, Inuyama was decommissioned and passed to Aichi Prefecture. Then, in October 1891, the Nōbi earthquake — magnitude eight and upwards, one of the largest inland quakes in Japanese history — brought down half the keep.

The prefecture had no means of rebuilding it. So in 1895 the castle was handed to the Naruse family, the former lords, on one condition: that they repair it. The Naruse and the townspeople raised the money between them, and the keep went back up.

The Naruse had been here since 1617, when Naruse Masanari, senior retainer to the Owari branch of the Tokugawa, took the castle and gave the keep the shape it has now. His descendants held it until the Restoration and, through this arrangement, well beyond — Inuyama spent roughly a century as **the only privately owned castle in Japan**, an oddity that ended only in 2004, when ownership passed to the family's foundation to secure its long-term conservation.

In between came the second blow. The Ise Bay typhoon of 1959 stripped tiles and tore off plaster, and the keep went through four years of dismantling and repair to reach the condition it is in today.

Two disasters, two rescues, both funded by the family and the town rather than the state. It is a modest history compared with Himeji's brushes with fire and bureaucracy, and in some ways a more affecting one.

## The name, and the man who gave it

The castle's alternative name, Hakutei-jō — White Emperor Castle — was reportedly bestowed by the Confucian scholar Ogyū Sorai in the mid-Edo period, after the Baidicheng of Li Bai's poem on the Yangtze. Sorai was casting the Kiso as the Yangtze, which is flattering to the Kiso, and telling you something about the aesthetic self-image of the educated class of the day. It stuck.

## The town is half the point

Do not treat Inuyama as a castle with a car park. The town below it is unusually intact, and an afternoon there is the better half of the visit.

The street running south from the hill is lined with old merchants' houses and given over, cheerfully, to eating on foot: *gohei-mochi*, pounded rice on a skewer with a sweet miso glaze, grilled to order; skewered everything else; *miso dengaku*, which is what the Tōkai region does instead of subtlety.

At the foot of the hill, Sankō Inari shrine runs a corridor of vermilion torii up the slope. Look at the pillars: one carries the Naruse name, the family from the castle above. The shrine and the fortress were never separate institutions.

And then there is Urakuen, where you will find **Jo-an** — a tea house of the early seventeenth century, built by Oda Urakusai, Nobunaga's younger brother, and itself a National Treasure. A National Treasure tea house and a National Treasure keep in the same small town is a combination that exists nowhere else in Japan. Jo-an is tiny, exacting, and viewed under conditions; access is by advance arrangement and the garden closes on certain days, so check before you build a day around it.

If you can time a visit to early April, the Inuyama Matsuri brings out tall festival floats carrying *karakuri* automata, worked by hand from inside, through streets they were built to fit. It is an Important Intangible Folk Cultural Property and part of the UNESCO-listed Yama, Hoko, Yatai float festivals — and it is, more usefully, a very good night out.

## The Particulars

**Getting there** — Meitetsu to Inuyama or Inuyama-yūen; about fifteen minutes on foot from either. Roughly 25–30 minutes from Meitetsu Nagoya.

**Admission and hours** — Open 9am to 5pm, last entry 4.30pm; ¥1,000 for adults as of the 2026 revision. Prices change: check [inuyama-castle.jp](https://inuyama-castle.jp/) before travelling.

**How long** — An hour for the keep, two with the old town, half a day if you add Urakuen.

**The climb** — Short but genuinely steep, and the internal stairs are close to ladders. Soft shoes, and a bag you can wear on your back.

**When** — Autumn for the walk through town; winter for the clearest view from the balcony; early April if you want the festival.

**Nearby** — Urakuen and the Jo-an tea house (book ahead); Sankō Inari and Haritsuna shrines; the castle-town street, which is where lunch is.
