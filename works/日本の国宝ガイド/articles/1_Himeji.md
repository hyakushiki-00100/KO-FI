# The Castle That Is Always Being Made New

*Japan's most photographed fortress is not a survivor so much as a four-hundred-year habit of repair. Once you see that, Himeji stops being a postcard.*

---

When the last of the scaffolding came off Himeji Castle in March 2015, a good number of Japanese visitors complained that it was too white.

They had a point. Six years and some ¥2.8 billion had gone into the great keep, and the new lime plaster — laid over every surface, down to the joints between the roof tiles — came up luminous, faintly synthetic, a shade that flattened badly under the grey skies of a Harima winter. People had grown fond of the weathered version. They wanted their old castle back.

The complaint has the building precisely the wrong way round. Himeji is white because someone has recently made it white, and someone has been making it white, on and off, since 1609. The castle has not stood for four centuries; it has been *kept* standing for four centuries, at considerable expense, by people who mostly never met one another. That distinction is the whole pleasure of the place, and it is worth carrying up the hill with you.

## Three men and a granddaughter

A fort went up on this hill in 1333, and over the following centuries thirteen families and forty-eight lords passed through — Himeji sits on the road west, and whoever held it had a say in the country's affairs.

Three of those men made the castle you see. Hideyoshi built a three-storey keep here as a base for his western campaigns. Ikeda Terumasa, installed after Sekigahara, spent eight years from 1601 replacing it with the five-tiered, six-storey structure that survives. And Honda Tadamasa, arriving in 1617, laid out the western bailey — for reasons that were entirely domestic.

Tadamasa's son had married Princess Sen, granddaughter of Ieyasu, widowed at Osaka when Toyotomi Hideyori died in the summer siege. She came to Himeji as a young woman starting again. The cosmetic turret and the long gallery known as the Hundred-Ken Corridor were built for her household, and walking them now — polished floorboards, a run of small windows, the drilled quiet of a building nobody needs any more — is the one part of the castle with no military logic to it at all. Worth the detour precisely for that.

## Two near-misses

Himeji has twice come close to disappearing, and neither time had anything to do with war.

The first was bureaucratic. After the Restoration the castle passed to the army, an ordinance of 1872 sorted the country's fortresses into keep-or-demolish, and although Himeji was marked for keeping, the maintenance bills were ruinous and the paperwork for demolition got some way along. It was stopped by an intervention at court during an imperial progress. There is also a durable story that the castle went to auction and sold for a sum in the region of ¥23.50 — the figure varies with the teller — and was left standing only because carting away the tiles would have cost more than the timber was worth. Treat it as folklore with a plausible core.

The second was fire. On 22 June 1945, and again through the night of 3–4 July, Himeji was bombed. The city burned; more than five hundred people died. The castle did not burn. An incendiary is said to have come through the keep and failed to detonate; the building is also said to have been draped in dark netting. Nobody knows. What is documented is the effect: a white tower standing over several square kilometres of ash, which people who were there described, without embarrassment, as the reason they kept going.

## The dismantling

Then came the repairs that make the castle what it is.

From 1956, the Shōwa restoration took the keep down — not patched it, *took it down*, every timber, and put it back. Two great pillars run from the basement to the top floor; the eastern one had gone thirty-seven centimetres out of true, and the western one was rotten to the core and had to be replaced outright with new stock. The bill came to around ¥530 million. The Heisei work of 2009 to 2015 did the plaster, the roof and the seismic strengthening, and produced the whiteness that upset everybody.

Stand in the courtyard and do the arithmetic: almost nothing you are looking at has been left alone for more than a couple of generations. This is not a ruin that endured. It is a commitment that has been renewed, repeatedly, by people who could have spent the money on something else.

## How to read the walls

Two things reward attention.

The first is the plan of the keep. Himeji is a *renritsu-shiki* castle — one great tower with three smaller ones, all tied together by roofed corridors into a ring. The prettiness is a by-product. The ring encloses a courtyard, and anyone who fought their way in found himself standing in it, being shot at from four sides. It is the rare piece of architecture that is beautiful and homicidal in the same gesture.

The second is the loopholes. There are 997 of them in the inner baileys, and they are not all the same shape: 844 circles, triangles and squares for firearms, and 153 tall, narrow rectangles for bows. The reason is pleasingly practical — you need vertical room to draw a bow. Once someone tells you this you cannot walk past a wall here without reading it, which is the best thing a guide can do for you.

The approach is worth the same attention. The path from the main gate turns, and turns again, and never lets you walk at the tower for more than a few seconds. You are being slowed down and led under things. An early-Meiji bird's-eye painting of the castle makes the logic instantly legible in a way no photograph from ground level ever will — the moats folded around each other, the baileys stepping sideways towards the keep.

## Doing it properly

Two hours covers the keep and the western bailey at a civilised pace. Three to four lets you look at gates, turrets and stonework, and take in Koko-en next door. In cherry-blossom season and over the long holidays there is a genuine queue for the keep, and the only real answer is to be at the gate when it opens.

Koko-en, laid out on the site of the lord's west residence, is nine gardens in one wall — a pond circuit, a tea house, a restaurant — and it is where to go when the castle has worn you out. Autumn there is very good indeed.

As for lunch: Himeji does oden with grated ginger in soy rather than mustard, which sounds minor and is not. The Harima Sea is conger country, and *densuke anago*, the outsized local variety, is the thing to order if it appears on a menu. Neither is refined. Both are correct.

## The Particulars

**Getting there** — Himeji is a Shinkansen stop; the castle is a twenty-minute walk straight up Ōtemae-dōri from the north exit, visible the whole way. Buses run to Ōtemon-mae, five minutes on foot from the gate.

**Admission and hours** — Open from 9am, last entry 4pm; ¥1,000 for adults, with a combined ticket for Koko-en. Fees and opening hours change; check the official site before you travel: [city.himeji.lg.jp/castle](https://www.city.himeji.lg.jp/castle/)

**How long** — Two hours for the essentials, four to do it justice.

**When** — Spring for the blossom and the crowds, autumn for Koko-en, winter for clear air and a hard-edged silhouette.

**Nearby** — Koko-en garden; the Himeji City Museum of Art and the Hyōgo Prefectural Museum of History, both within a short walk, for context.
