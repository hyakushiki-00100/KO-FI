# A Castle Made of Other Castles

*Hikone was assembled in the early 1600s from the salvaged parts of half the fortresses in the region. Four centuries on, nobody is entirely sure where all of it came from.*

---

Some years ago, conservators took the Taiko-mon apart.

The drum gate stands on the approach to Hikone's main bailey, and the work was routine — dismantle, survey the timbers, repair, reassemble. What the survey found was that the gate had been a gate somewhere else first. The joinery had been cut for a different building on a different site and then reused here. That much was clear from the wood itself.

What the survey could not establish, and what nobody has established since, is which castle it came from.

This is the correct introduction to Hikone, because the Taiko-mon is not an anomaly. It is the house style. Almost every significant structure on this hill is said to have arrived from somewhere else: the keep from Ōtsu, seat of Kyōgoku Takatsugu; the Tenbin turret from Nagahama; the three-storey turret in the western bailey from Odani; the Sawaguchi corridor turret, now gone, from Sawayama. Hikone is a castle built out of other castles, and it is far more interesting for it.

## Red armour and an inheritance

The man who should have built it never did. Ii Naomasa, one of the four generals closest to Ieyasu and the commander whose troops fought in the lacquered scarlet armour that made the Ii name, was rewarded after Sekigahara with Sawayama — the confiscated seat of his defeated opponent Ishida Mitsunari. He took a wound at Sekigahara that never properly healed, and died in 1602.

His son Ii Naotsugu chose a new hill, Hikone-yama, and began work in 1603. The castle was substantially complete by about 1607.

The timing explains everything about how it was built. This was the great age of shogunal levy construction, the countryside was full of fortresses that had just been decommissioned, and the sensible thing — the thing everybody did — was to take the good timber from castles nobody needed any more and put it into the castle they did. Hikone was not cut corners. It was competent procurement, and it required real skill: parts cut for other buildings, on other stone, to other dimensions, had to be persuaded into a single coherent fortress. That they behave as one building is the achievement.

There is a pleasing side effect. Because the region's other castles are gone, Hikone is now the only place several of them still physically exist. The hill is an archive of vanished architecture, held together as a working structure.

The house was settled a decade later. Naotsugu's younger brother, **Ii Naotaka**, distinguished himself at the winter and summer sieges of Osaka in 1614–15; the headship passed to him, and the Ii held Hikone until the Restoration.

## Small, and dense with ornament

The keep is a modest thing — three roof tiers, three storeys, about twenty metres including the stone base — and at first glance it looks slight next to Himeji.

Then you look properly. The elevation is packed with gables: straight-sided *kirizuma*, hipped-and-gabled *irimoya*, and the swooping curve of the *kara-hafu*, layered against one another so that the eye never settles. The first and second floors carry *katōmado*, the flower-headed windows borrowed from temple architecture. Nothing is left plain.

The strategy is compensation, and it works. Himeji impresses through scale and calm; Hikone has neither in quantity, so it spends its budget on incident. Small buildings that know they are small, and answer with density of detail, tend to age well. This one has.

## The best structure on the hill

The keep is not the finest thing at Hikone. The **Tenbin yagura** is.

It sits across a cut ravine, reached by a bridge, and consists of two two-storey corner turrets, mirror images, joined by a long low corridor turret — the shape of a balance scale, which is what its name says. It is genuinely handsome, symmetrical in a way Japanese castle architecture rarely bothers to be.

It is also the sharpest piece of defensive thinking on the site. Drop the bridge and the main bailey becomes an island, cut off by the ravine, with the twin turrets looking down into it. Beauty and function are not in tension here; they are the same decision seen from two sides.

Worth noting, too, how much else survives. Most Japanese castles retain a keep and very little else. Hikone still has its Tenbin turret, its drum gate, its western three-storey turret, and — unusually — its inner and middle moats more or less complete. You can read the whole organism, not just the head.

## The scaffolding of 1878

Hikone came within days of being firewood.

After the Restoration the castle passed to the army, then towards private sale and demolition, and by October 1878 the scaffolding for taking down the keep had gone up. What saved it was an accident of the calendar: the Meiji Emperor, returning from a progress through the northern provinces, stopped nearby. A member of the party — most accounts name Ōkuma Shigenobu, though the attribution is disputed and other versions credit a relative of the emperor — went to look at the castle, learned what was about to happen to it, and argued for a reprieve. The order came down and the scaffolding came off.

Set this beside Himeji, saved by an intervention at court, and Matsue, bought back out of two men's own pockets, and a pattern emerges that ought to trouble anyone comfortable with the idea that great buildings look after themselves. Three of Japan's five National Treasure keeps are here because one person, on one particular day, said something.

## The garden, which is not an afterthought

Below the north face lies **Genkyū-en**, laid out in the late 1670s under the fourth lord, Ii Naooki, and reworked in 1813 as a retirement villa for the eleventh. It is a circuit garden around a pond, composed after the Eight Views of Ōmi — the local canon of Lake Biwa scenery, itself modelled on the Chinese Eight Views of Xiao and Xiang. Layers of quotation, in other words, which is very much the taste of the period.

Its purpose, structurally, is the borrowed view: walk the far side of the pond and the keep appears above the trees with its reflection beneath it. This is the picture of Hikone, and the reason to leave an hour that you might otherwise have spent on the walls. Go in late autumn if you can.

## Practical civility

Two hours of the hill and the garden is enough; three if you take in the Hikone Castle Museum, which holds the Ii family's armour and can show you the red *akazonae* harness as an object rather than a legend.

Below the Kyōbashi gate, Yumekyōbashi Castle Road runs on in white plaster and black lattice — an exercise in coordinated townscape rather than a survival, but a well-judged one, and where you will eat. Ōmi beef is the region's calling card. Look also for *aka-konnyaku*, the local konjac dyed red with iron oxide, firm and clean-tasting and entirely without the funk the material usually carries; and *hikone-don*, in which slow-cooked Ōmi beef tendon and shredded red konjac go over rice under a soft egg. It is a modern civic invention rather than an ancient dish, and none the worse for it.

One further note, offered without comment: the castle employs a mascot, a white cat in a samurai helmet, who appears three times a day and commands crowds that the Tenbin yagura does not. Consult the schedule and plan accordingly, in whichever direction you prefer.

## The Particulars

**Getting there** — JR to Hikone; fifteen minutes on foot from the west exit. Five minutes by train from Maibara, which puts it within easy reach of the Shinkansen.

**Admission and hours** — Open 8.30am to 5pm, last entry 4.30pm; ¥1,000 for adults, ¥300 for children, the ticket covering Genkyū-en. Verify before travelling: [hikonecastle.com](https://hikonecastle.com/)

**How long** — An hour for the keep alone, ninety minutes to two hours with the garden, three with the museum.

**When** — Late autumn, for the maples reflected in the Genkyū-en pond; winter for hard light on the gables.

**Nearby** — Genkyū-en; the Hikone Castle Museum; Yumekyōbashi Castle Road for lunch; Shibanchō Square for early-twentieth-century western-style architecture.
